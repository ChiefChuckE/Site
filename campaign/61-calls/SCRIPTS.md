## Scripts — Phase 1 (ready to record)

### CALL 1 · Nobody is built for this
**On screen:** Nobody is built for this job.

Nobody is built for this job.
Not me. Not you. Not the toughest one on your crew.

I spent my career with CAL FIRE. I led people into the worst days of other people's lives.

We see things the human brain was never meant to see.
Then we go home and pass the potatoes like nothing happened.

That's not strength. That's a slow leak.

For the next 61 days, one video a day, I'm going to tell you what nobody told me.
About PTSD. What it does to us. What actually helps.

No sugar coating.

If you wear the uniform, or love someone who does, stay.

*Pinned comment question:* What's one thing you wish someone told you your first year?

---

### CALL 2 · It's an injury
**On screen:** PTSD is not weakness. It's an injury.

If you broke your leg on a call, you'd get it set. Nobody would call you weak.

PTSD is an injury too. You just can't see it on an X-ray.

Trauma changes how your brain works.
The part that spots danger gets louder.
The part that says "you're safe now" gets quieter.

That's not a character flaw. That's biology.

And here's what matters. Injuries heal. PTSD is treatable. People get better.

But only after you stop calling it weakness
and start calling it what it is.

An injury. From the job.

*Pinned comment question:* What did your department call it when someone was struggling?

---

### CALL 3 · It's the pile
**On screen:** It's not always one bad call. It's the pile.

Most people think PTSD comes from one horrible call. Sometimes it does.

But for a lot of us, it's the pile.

The kid. The rollover. The man nobody found for a week. The one you worked for forty minutes.
Year after year after year.

Every one goes in the bag. You tell yourself you're fine.
The bag doesn't care.

The doctors who wrote the official rules for PTSD put us right in there.
Repeated exposure to the worst details. They named first responders.

So if no single call broke you, that doesn't mean nothing's wrong.

It means the bag got full.

*Pinned comment question:* How many years in before you noticed the weight?

---

### CALL 4 · The alarm stuck on
**On screen:** Your brain has a smoke detector. Trauma jams it on.

Your brain has a smoke detector. It's called the amygdala.

Its job is simple. Spot danger. Sound the alarm. Flood you with adrenaline.
On a call, that saves your life.

Trauma can jam that detector in the ON position.

Now it goes off in the grocery store. At your kid's game.
At two in the morning when nothing's happening.

Your heart pounds. You scan every room. You snap at the people you love.

You're not crazy. Your alarm is stuck.

And you don't fix an alarm by yelling at it.
You get it serviced.

That's what treatment does.

*Pinned comment question:* Where does your alarm go off when it shouldn't?

---

### CALL 5 · The four signs
**On screen:** 4 signs of PTSD. Learn them.

Four signs. Learn them like your ABCs.

One. It comes back. Nightmares. Flashbacks. A smell that drops you right back on scene.

Two. You avoid. Certain streets. Certain calls. Talking about it at all.

Three. Your mood and thinking change. Guilt. Numb. "The world is rotten." You stop enjoying what you loved.

Four. You're always on. Jumpy. Angry. Can't sleep. Back to the wall.

If those hang on for more than a month and get in the way of your life,
that's not "just the job."

That's a reason to get checked.

*Pinned comment question:* Which of the four did you see first, in yourself or someone else?

### CALL 6 · It's not "just a dream"
**On screen:** "It's just a bad dream." No it isn't.

"It's just a bad dream." No it isn't.

When the same call plays at three a.m. Again. And again.
That's your brain trying to file something it can't file.

Same with flashbacks. You're awake. You're at the store.
And for a second, you're back on that scene. You can smell it.

That's called intrusion. It's one of the core signs of PTSD.

A lot of us handle it by not sleeping.
Or by drinking until we pass out instead of falling asleep.

Neither works for long.

Nightmares are treatable. There are therapies built just for them.
Tell a doctor. Use the word "nightmares." Out loud.

*Pinned comment question:* Have you ever told a doctor about your nightmares? Yes or no.

### CALL 7 · Avoidance looks like "fine"
**On screen:** The most dangerous symptom of PTSD looks like you're fine.

The most dangerous symptom of PTSD looks like you're fine.

It's called avoidance.

You take the long way home so you don't pass that intersection.
You trade off certain calls. You change the channel.
When someone asks, you say "I'm good" and change the subject.

It works. For a while.

But every time you avoid it, your brain learns the memory is still dangerous.
The fence gets taller. Your world gets smaller.

Ask yourself one honest question today.
What am I going out of my way not to think about?

That's where the work is.

*Pinned comment question:* "I'm good." How many times did you say it this week?

### CALL 8 · Numb is not tough
**On screen:** Your kid hits a home run and you feel nothing.

Your kid hits a home run, and you feel nothing.

Your wife says "I love you," and you say it back on autopilot.

We call that tough. Steady.

It's not. It's numb.

On a call, shutting your feelings off is a tool. It lets you work.
The problem is, there's no switch that only turns off the bad ones.

Turn off the horror, and you turn off the joy too.

If home feels flat. Gray. Far away.
That's a sign. Not of weakness. Of an injury that needs care.

The people at your table deserve the real you.
So do you.

*Pinned comment question:* When's the last time you really felt something good?

### CALL 9 · Back to the wall
**On screen:** You picked the seat facing the door. Didn't you.

You walk into a restaurant. You pick the seat facing the door.
You clock the exits. You watch the guy by the counter.

You call it awareness. Everybody on the job does it.

But when you can't turn it off. At home. On vacation.
In bed at two a.m., listening for a noise.
That's hypervigilance. It's one of the signs of PTSD.

Your body is burning fuel like it's on a call, twenty-four seven.
Nobody runs like that forever.

It shows up as exhaustion. A short fuse. A body that's always tight.

Being ready is the job.
Never standing down is the injury.

*Pinned comment question:* Be honest. Where do you sit in a restaurant?

### CALL 10 · Stress, burnout, PTSD
**On screen:** A vacation won't fix PTSD.

Stress. Burnout. PTSD. People use them like they're the same thing. They're not.

Stress is the weight. It goes up on a bad shift. It comes back down after.

Burnout is running on empty. Tired. Cynical. You stop caring about the work.

PTSD is tied to trauma. The memories come back. You avoid. You're always on edge.
Your mood and thinking change.
And it doesn't go away after a few days off.

Here's the hard truth. You can have all three at once.

And a vacation won't fix PTSD.

Know which one you're fighting. Then fight it with the right tool.

*Pinned comment question:* Which one are you fighting right now? Stress, burnout, or PTSD?

### CALL 11 · Two uniforms (Veterans Day)
**On screen:** Two uniforms. Same wound.

Today is Veterans Day.

A lot of us wore one uniform before we wore this one.
Military first. Then the fire service. The badge. The ambulance.

That's two tours of trauma. One overseas. One on your own streets.

And two cultures that said the same thing. Don't complain. Handle it.

If you're a vet who became a responder, you've been carrying a double load.
That's not a reason for shame. It's a reason to get help that understands both.

Veterans, the VA treats PTSD. Crisis line: dial 988, then press 1.

Thank you for both of your services.

### CALL 12 · The smell
**On screen:** It's not always what you saw. It's what you smelled.

Ask any responder. It's not always what you saw.
It's what you smelled.

There's a reason. Smell takes a shortcut straight to the parts of your brain that handle fear and memory.

So years later, a whiff of something. Burnt hair. Diesel. A certain cleaner.
And you're back there. Heart racing. Before you even know why.

That's a trigger. It's not weakness. It's wiring.

Here's one thing to try. When it hits, name it out loud.
"That's a trigger. I'm in my kitchen. It's Tuesday."

Naming it tells your brain: not now. Not here.

*Pinned comment question:* No details. Just yes or no: is there a smell that takes you back?

There's a responder who hears it all and never sees how it ends.

The dispatcher.

The mother screaming that her baby's not breathing.

Dispatchers get PTSD too. They're first responders,

If you've ever heard their voice in your ear on a call, thank them. Then check on them.

Dispatchers, every one of these 61 is for you too.
